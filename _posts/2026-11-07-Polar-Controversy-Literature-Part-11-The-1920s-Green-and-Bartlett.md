When Peary died in 1920, shortly before Captain Hall published his “Volume II,” there was an outpouring of effusive obituaries, and memorial tributes from his friends were held at Bowdoin College and the Explorers Club. He was laid to rest with full military honors in Arlington National Cemetery, attended by many high officials of the US Government, including the Vice President and Chief Justice.  
An imposing monument was soon erected over his grave, courtesy of the National Geographic Society.

After Peary’s death, Herbert Bridgman toyed with the idea of writing a biography of Peary, but was cut short by his own death in 1924. Instead, the Peary family authorized it to be written by Commander Fitzhugh Green.

![Fitzhugh Green](http://babryce.com/cooktest/assets/Fitzhugh-Green_hzBa.jpg "Fitzhugh Green")

Green had been on Donald MacMillan’s Crocker Land Expedition of 1913-1917. That expedition’s main goal was to explore that land which Peary had seen far at sea from the heights of Axel Heiberg Island in 1906. Green had accompanied MacMillan on the initial trek across the polar pack to locate Crocker Land’s exact position in 1914, but instead proved that Crocker Land had no position, because, in fact, it did not exist. They excused Peary’s “mistake” in reporting its sighting by saying he had been deceived by a Fata Morgana, a mirage of the sea ice. (see the author’s book, The Crocker Land Diaries, available on Amazon.com.  See the post for December 18, 2025).

![Peary the man who refused to fail](http://babryce.com/cooktest/assets/Peary-the-man-who-refused-to-fail_hzBa.avif "Peary the man who refused to fail")

Green’s biography appeared in 1925, titled *Peary: The Man who refused to fail.* Although its proofs and galleys were all checked by the Peary family, it proved to be a maudlin affair that one reviewer called “a hodgepodge of sentimental slush.” For instance, its readers learned of Peary’s origins and birth: “It is as though that group of good people were laid like an egg of Destiny in the womb of a clean nature for the single purpose of bringing forth a finer specimen of the race than the commonplace mechanics of gestation could achieve.”

In the book’s introduction, by Peary’s widow, Josephine, she wrote, “It is my hope that this work will create a clearer and better understanding of a character that is too little known or appreciated.” Instead of fulfilling Jo Peary’s hope, Green felt compelled to explain why “many in Peary’s command used to return hating him in a way that murder couldn’t gratify.” In so doing, he had left much space for reading between his lines, often refuting, among other things, charges that Peary was “inhuman,” cold, greedy, venial, unsympathetic, ungenerous, and that he had a mania for fame.

As to the Polar Controversy, Green added little to what had already been discussed elsewhere, and made no defense of any of the evidence against Peary’s claim. “The story of the Cook-Peary controversy,” he wrote in his brief chapter on the subject, “needs no explanation or amendment here,” and described it as “of no consequence,” other than an illustration of all Peary was made to suffer as a consequence of the triumphant culmination of this 20 years of painful Arctic experiences. However, later in the book, intending to show an example of one of the many “injustices” he had to endure because of it, he selected the process by which he gained the approval of the National Geographic Society, which actually was ridiculously lax in its examination of his original records and instruments. This was especially ludicrous in that the mistaken notion that the NGS was the equivalent of the Royal Geographical Society in Great Britain, rather than no more than a private publishing concern with no official connection with the United States Government, led to his claims acceptance worldwide without any further official examination. Green then injudiciously went on to quote some of the damning testimony he gave before the Naval Affairs Committee in 1911, which up to then had not gained much public notice outside of Captain Thomas Hall’s books.

Meanwhile the copies of Captain Hall’s book that he had deposited in those many libraries in 1917, began to have their effect. Magazine articles questioning Peary’s claim, most prominently those written by William Shea and Henshaw Ward for the *Independent*, and both contemplated books of their own elaborating what they found in Captain Hall’s, but neither of them was ever published.

![The Log of Bob Bartlett](http://babryce.com/cooktest/assets/The-Log-of-Bob-Bartlett1-767x1024_hzBa.jpg "The Log of Bob Bartlett")

In 1928, Bob Bartlett, captain of Peary’s ship Roosevelt, who had accompanied Peary over the ice on his last polar attempt 1909, but was turned back by Peary at what he claimed to be a point 133 nmi. from the North Pole, published his autobiography under the title *The Log of Bob Bartlett.* Through faults of memory of events now 20 years in the past, or deliberate distortion, Bartlett’s book falls far short of documented events in more than a few places. Perhaps the best assessment Bartlett’s book was written by The Reverend J. Gordon Hayes as a postscript to his book on Peary that came out in Great Britain a year later in 1929:

“Something more than loyalty to his old chief, however is necessary to prove that [Peary] reached the Pole. As no new data are given, this breezy book fails to help the cause that its author has at heart. He gives nothing bearing on the crux of the problem except personal opinion; he adds little to the information given in Commander Green’s biography, and he is like Commander Green in giving a wide berth to all the real difficulties. . . .[So] Here is a second book , published, like the biography, with the evident purpose of propaganda. Yet, for the second time, an opportunity of proving Peary’s claim has been lost. . . .The proofs that he did not reach the Pole are totally unaffected. . . . The biography, indeed, as the penultimate word of his supporters, was enlightening, though not, perhaps altogether in the manner intended. Should the Log of Bob Bartlett be the last word on behalf of that claim, [the evidence showing that Peary did not reach the Pole] may prove to be the verdict of history. . . . One sidelight is unwittingly thrown on the central figure when Bartlett says that Peary “knew more about red tape than any three average men,” and so “he didn’t have much trouble fixing me up with a job.” , . . This was how Peary managed to get his claim to the Pole ‘fixed up.’”

The Reverend Hayes was a keen student of Polar subjects, and the book in which this critique appeared was about to become a potent factor in raising doubts, and convincing many in Europe, that Peary’s claim to the North Pole was simply invalid.
